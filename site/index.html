First time? Here's what to click.

On a Mac

  1. Double-click SETUP. When macOS says it can't verify it, click Done.
  2. Open System Settings > Privacy & Security, scroll down and click Open Anyway. You only do this once.
  3. If it asks whether Terminal may use files on a removable volume, click Allow.

On Windows

  1. Right-click the ZIP, choose Extract All, then double-click SETUP-Windows in the new folder.
  2. If Windows says it protected your PC, click More info, then Run anyway.

On Linux, run bash SETUP.command in a terminal. It needs python3 and curl.

Questions


Before you set it up.






What is Tinfoil Kit?
Tinfoil Kit is a free, open-source kit for when the internet goes down: an AI that runs on your laptop, all of Wikipedia, medical and repair guides, and maps with directions, kept on the laptop itself or on an external drive. You set it up while you're online. After that, it all works with no internet.


Is the AI as good as ChatGPT?
Not quite. It's an open model small enough to run on a laptop, so it's a step behind the biggest cloud AIs. It's good at explaining and summarizing, it answers your questions from the library with sources, and none of it needs the internet.


Do I need the AI?
No. The library and the map work without it, and with the AI off, a question still searches every book and shows the best passages, with links to the full pages. To leave it out entirely, pick No AI in the kit builder: the same books and maps, with no models to download.


What do I need?
A Mac with an M chip (M1 or newer), or a Windows or Linux PC with an Intel or AMD chip. The kit goes on your laptop's own disk or on an external SSD, and a drive formatted exFAT starts on all three. 32 GB of memory runs the full-size AI; with less, you get a lighter one. The recommended kit is about 300 GB, and the kit builder above shows the total for your picks. Short on room? The installer offers just the essentials, about 30 to 50 GB: the AI, the map, and the medical, preparedness and repair books. On your laptop's own disk, the kit always leaves 50 GB free.


Which AI will my laptop run?
It depends on how much memory it has. The kit picks for you, and you can switch models any time.
MemoryThe AI it runs
8 GBGemma 4 E4B, the small model (5 GB). Close other apps before you ask it something.
16 to 24 GBGemma 4 12B, the mid-size model (7 GB). It reads photos too.
32 GB or moreInkling 2 Mini, the default (18 GB). Qwen3.8 27B and Gemma 4 26B fit too, for a second opinion.
The installer says which one your laptop runs, and asks before downloading the models too big for it: skipping them saves about 50 GB on a 16 or 24 GB laptop. To see your memory, open About This Mac, or Settings > System > About on Windows. On a Windows or Linux PC the AI runs on the graphics card, so without a good one, use Gemma 4 E4B.


Does anything leave my laptop?
No. Once the kit is filled, every part runs on your laptop at 127.0.0.1 with no account and no telemetry. The only time it goes online is when you install or update it, and then it only talks to the places it downloads from: GitHub, Kiwix, Hugging Face, Protomaps, Geofabrik, GraphHopper, Adoptium, Maven Central and npm, plus nuget.org for Python on Windows and any source you add yourself.


What does setup change on my computer?
Very little. The models, books, maps and programs all go in one folder, TinfoilKit, in your home folder or on the drive, and nothing runs unless you start it. Besides that:
  • Windows: the installer keeps python.org's Python (about 15 MB) in your user folder, since the kit's tools run on Python and Windows doesn't include it.
  • Mac: macOS asks you once to allow the installer, since it's a script from the internet rather than an App Store app. On a Mac without Python, the installer keeps one for the kit's tools (about 25 MB) in your Library folder.
  • Mac: the kit lifts macOS's "downloaded from the internet" mark from its own files, and signs a downloaded program on your Mac if it won't start otherwise, so everything opens with a double-click in an outage.
  • Mac: a kit in your home folder stays out of Time Machine backups, and a kit drive stays out of Spotlight.
  • Linux: if python3 or curl is missing, the installer says how to install them.
Two more happen only if you ask: a home server can start the kit with the computer and share it on your home network behind an access code. Uninstalling deletes the folder and undoes the rest, including the Python the installer got.


How do I know it's safe?
Every line is open to read, and the kit is built so you don't have to trust a server. It installs updates to its own code only if they're signed with the maintainer's key, which never touches GitHub. Every model, book, map and program comes straight from its publisher, checked against the publisher's checksum wherever one is posted. Its servers answer only your own computer unless you turn on sharing, which asks for a code. The README's Security section says what it protects against, what it can't, and how to report a problem.


What's the catch?
None. It's open source under the MIT license, with no account, no telemetry in the kit and no analytics on this site. It's a side project from the team behind DoTheySupportIt. What it costs you is disk space and a few hours of downloading.


Can I trust the AI's answers?
Check it, the way you'd check a well-read friend. When you ask a question, it answers from the library and numbers its sources, so you can open the page it used. For anything medical, the kit is a place to look things up, in WikiMed and the Army's medical manuals. It isn't a doctor: read the source, and get trained help when you can.


What's under the hood?
Open-source parts, all served on your laptop at 127.0.0.1: llama.cpp's llama-server runs the AI, kiwix-serve runs the library, and Protomaps, Photon and GraphHopper run the map. One list in the kit says where every model, book and map comes from, and each update checks every download against its publisher's checksum and keeps the old version until the new one verifies. The README has the commands.


Can my family use it from their phones?
Yes. Sharing stays off until you turn it on. Then phones and laptops on your Wi-Fi or hotspot open the address it shows and type its 8-digit access code, or scan its QR code. No internet is needed, only a local network, and other devices and websites can't use it without the code. It works on a Mac, Windows and Linux, and a computer that stays on can share it all the time, as a home server. The guide shows how to turn it on.


How long does it run on battery?
The big model uses a lot of power: expect a couple of hours of active use on a laptop. The small model uses much less, and reading the library with the AI off uses almost nothing. A USB-C power station and a folding solar panel keep it going.


Does it run on Windows or Linux?
Yes, all of it: the AI, the library and the map. Download and open it on any of the three. A kit on an exFAT drive starts on a Mac, Windows or Linux, whichever one set it up. Without a strong graphics card the AI is slower. Windows and Linux are newer to the kit, so they're tested less than the Mac.


How do I update it?
Whenever you like, while you're online, open the installer again. There's no schedule. It gets the newest version of the kit first, so models, books and maps added since arrive too, and the picks you made stay. Only what changed downloads, and your old files keep working until the new ones check out.


How do I remove it?
Run the uninstaller in the kit's folder. Once you type delete, it removes the kit and everything it downloaded, and on a drive it leaves everything else alone.


Is all this content legal to download?
Yes, for your own use. The kit downloads everything straight from its publishers: Kiwix packages openly licensed sites like Wikipedia, WikiMed and iFixit, and US government guides, and the AI models are open-weight models published for anyone to run. Each keeps its own license, and some, like iFixit's, forbid commercial use, so don't sell drives you've filled. Every source and its license.
