Questions


Before you set it up.






What is Tinfoil Kit?
Tinfoil Kit is a free, open-source kit for when the internet goes down. It puts an AI, all of Wikipedia, medical and repair guides, and maps with directions on your laptop or an external drive. You set it up while you're online, and after that it all works with no internet.


Is the AI as good as ChatGPT?
Not quite. It's small enough to run on a laptop, so it's a step behind the big cloud AIs. It's good at explaining things, it shows where its answers come from, and it needs no internet.


Do I need the AI?
No. The books and the map work without it. With the AI off, a question still searches every book and shows the best passages. To leave it out, pick No AI in the kit builder above.


What do I need?
A Mac with an M chip (M1 or newer), or a Windows or Linux PC with an Intel or AMD chip. 32 GB of memory runs the full-size AI; with less, you get a lighter one. The recommended kit takes about 300 GB on your laptop or an external SSD, and the kit builder above shows the size of yours. Short on room? The installer can get just the essentials, about 30 to 50 GB: the AI, the map, and the medical, preparedness and repair books.


Which AI will my laptop run?
It depends on your laptop's memory. The kit picks for you, and you can switch any time.
MemoryThe AI it runs
8 GBGemma 4 E4B, the small model (5 GB). Close other apps before you ask it something.
16 to 24 GBGemma 4 12B, the mid-size model (7 GB). It reads photos too.
32 GB or moreInkling 2 Mini, the default (18 GB). Qwen3.8 27B and Gemma 4 26B fit too, for a second opinion.
The installer says which one your laptop runs, and offers to skip the ones too big for it, which saves about 50 GB on a 16 or 24 GB laptop. To see your memory, open About This Mac, or Settings > System > About on Windows. A Windows or Linux PC without a good graphics card should use Gemma 4 E4B.


Does anything leave my laptop?
No. Once it's set up, everything runs on your laptop, with no account and no tracking. It only goes online when you install or update it, and then it only talks to the places it downloads from: GitHub, Kiwix, Hugging Face, Protomaps, Geofabrik, GraphHopper, Adoptium, Maven Central and npm, plus nuget.org for Python on Windows and any source you add yourself.


What does setup change on my computer?
Very little. Everything goes in one folder, TinfoilKit, in your home folder or on the drive, and nothing runs unless you start it. Besides that:
  • Windows: the installer adds python.org's Python (about 15 MB) to your user folder, because the kit's tools need it.
  • Mac: the Tinfoil Kit app goes in your Applications folder. If your Mac has no Python, the installer adds one (about 25 MB) to your Library folder.
  • Mac: the kit clears macOS's "downloaded from the internet" mark from its own files, and signs a program if macOS won't open it otherwise, so everything opens in an outage.
  • Mac: a kit in your home folder stays out of Time Machine, and a kit drive stays out of Spotlight.
  • Linux: if python3 or curl is missing, the installer says how to get them.
Only if you ask: a home server starts the kit with the computer and shares it on your home network, with an access code. Uninstalling deletes the folder and undoes the rest, including the Python the installer added.


How do I know it's safe?
All the code is public, and you don't have to trust a server of ours. The kit installs updates to itself only if they're signed with the maintainer's key, which never touches GitHub. Every model, book, map and program comes straight from its publisher, checked against the publisher's checksum when there is one. Nothing outside your computer can reach the kit unless you turn on sharing, which asks for a code. The README's Security section says what it protects against, what it can't, and how to report a problem.


What's the catch?
None. It's free and open source (MIT license), with no account, no tracking in the kit and no analytics on this site. It's a side project from the team behind DoTheySupportIt. It costs you disk space and a few hours of downloading.


Can I trust the AI's answers?
Check it, like you'd check a well-read friend. Each answer shows the pages it came from, so you can read them yourself. For anything medical, use it to look things up in WikiMed and the Army's medical manuals. It isn't a doctor: read the source, and get trained help when you can.


What's under the hood?
Open-source parts, all running on your laptop: llama.cpp runs the AI, kiwix-serve runs the library, and Protomaps, Photon and GraphHopper run the map. One list in the kit says where every model, book and map comes from. Each update checks every download against its publisher's checksum, and keeps the old version until the new one checks out. The README has the commands.


Can my family use it from their phones?
Yes. Turn on sharing, and phones and laptops on your Wi-Fi or hotspot can open it: they scan its QR code or type its 8-digit access code. No internet is needed, just your local network, and nobody can use it without the code. A computer that stays on can share it all the time, as a home server. The guide shows how.


How long does it run on battery?
The big AI uses a lot of power: expect a couple of hours of active use. The small one uses much less, and reading with the AI off uses almost nothing. A USB-C power station and a folding solar panel keep it going.


Does it run on Windows or Linux?
Yes, all of it. Download and open it on any of the three. A kit on a drive formatted exFAT works on a Mac, Windows or Linux, whichever one set it up. Without a strong graphics card, the AI is slower. Windows and Linux are newer to the kit, so they're tested less than the Mac.


How do I update it?
Open the installer again whenever you're online. There's no schedule. New models, books and maps arrive, your picks stay, and only what changed downloads. Your old files keep working until the new ones check out.


How do I remove it?
Run the uninstaller in the kit's folder and type delete. It removes the kit and everything it downloaded. On a drive, it leaves everything else alone.


Is all this content legal to download?
Yes, for your own use. Everything comes straight from its publishers: openly licensed sites like Wikipedia, WikiMed and iFixit, US government guides, and AI models published for anyone to run. Each keeps its own license, and some, like iFixit's, forbid commercial use, so don't sell drives you've filled. Every source and its license.
